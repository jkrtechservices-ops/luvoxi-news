Plano vs Frisco: Which Texas Suburb Fits Your Lifestyle?

By Luvoxi News Editorial Team · Oct 18, 2026 · 5 min read

Plano and Frisco are two of the fastest‑growing suburbs of Dallas‑Fort Worth, but they differ in population size, earnings, housing costs and overall vibe. Understanding these metrics helps you choose the community that matches your budget, family needs, and career goals.

Key takeaways
  • Frisco earns more but costs more for homes and rent.
  • Plano offers slightly lower rent relative to income.
  • Both cities have high home‑ownership rates.
  • Frisco leans toward newer growth; Plano is larger and more established.

Population and Physical Size

Plano’s estimated population is 290,594, making it about 33% larger than Frisco’s 219,304 residents. The larger headcount translates into a more extensive urban footprint and a broader range of services. Frisco covers 62.4 square miles, with 61.8 square miles of land, and is known for rapid expansion since the early 2000s. Both cities sit within the Dallas‑Fort Worth metroplex, but Plano lies roughly 17 miles from downtown Dallas, while Frisco is about 25 miles from Dallas Love Field and DFW Airport.

Household Income and Economic Profile

Median household income in Frisco reaches $150,212, which is $37,959—or roughly 34 %—higher than Plano’s $112,253. This gap reflects Frisco’s rapid evolution into a corporate hub, with a concentration of newer professional opportunities tied to technology, finance, and logistics firms that have set up regional offices in the city’s sprawling business parks. In contrast, Plano’s economy leans on long‑standing headquarters such as Frito‑Lay, JCPenney, Pizza Hut, and Toyota Motor North America, keeping its income level well above the national average despite being lower than Frisco’s.

Both suburbs enjoy relatively affordable housing costs when measured against earnings. In Plano, the median gross rent of $1,841 per month represents about 20 % of the median household income, while Frisco’s median rent of $2,014 consumes roughly 16 % of its higher earnings. Home‑ownership also differs: 57 % of Plano’s occupied units are owner‑occupied, compared with 66 % in Frisco, and the median value of owner‑occupied homes is $465,900 in Plano versus $642,100 in Frisco—a 38 % premium that mirrors the city’s higher income base.

Population size adds another dimension: Plano’s 290,594 residents (about 33 % more than Frisco’s 219,304) create a larger tax base, yet Frisco’s younger median age of 38.3 years versus Plano’s 39.6 suggests a slightly newer workforce. Together, these economic indicators help prospective residents gauge which suburb aligns with their financial goals and lifestyle preferences.

Rent Prices and Affordability

The median gross rent in Plano sits at $1,841 per month, while Frisco’s median is $2,014—a $173 (about 9 %) increase. On the surface, Frisco’s higher rent might suggest a steeper cost of living, but the picture changes once household income is taken into account. Plano’s median household income is $112,253, meaning rent consumes roughly 20 % of a typical resident’s earnings. In Frisco, the median household income jumps to $150,212, so the same $2,014 rent represents only about 16 % of income. This lower rent‑to‑income ratio translates into a lighter housing burden for Frisco families, despite the higher dollar amount.

Affordability also reflects homeownership trends. About 57 % of occupied housing units in Plano are owner‑occupied, compared with 66 % in Frisco, indicating a stronger propensity to buy in the latter. The median value of owner‑occupied homes underscores this divide: Plano homes average $465,900, whereas Frisco’s median home price climbs to $642,100—roughly 38 % more expensive. Yet the higher incomes in Frisco help offset the price gap, making both renting and buying comparatively attainable for its residents. Ultimately, if you prioritize a lower rent‑to‑income percentage and a community where owning is more common, Frisco may feel more financially comfortable; if you prefer a slightly lower absolute rent figure and a larger rental market, Plano could be the better fit.

Home Values and Ownership Rates

Owner‑occupied homes in Plano carry a median value of $465,900, while Frisco’s median climbs to $642,100—a premium of roughly $176,200, or 38 % more. This price gap reflects Frisco’s newer, family‑oriented subdivisions and its higher household earnings. The median household income in Plano is $112,253, compared with $150,212 in Frisco, giving Frisco residents about $38 k (34 %) more purchasing power. Because of that extra income, a larger share of Frisco’s residents own their homes: 66 % of occupied housing units are owner‑occupied versus 57 % in Plano. The higher ownership rate also aligns with Frisco’s slightly younger median age (38.3 years) and its rapid growth—its 2020‑2024 ACS estimate puts the population at 219,304, about 33 % smaller than Plano’s 290,594 but still expanding quickly.

Renters feel the difference, too. Median gross rent in Plano is $1,841 per month, roughly 20 % of the city’s median income, while Frisco’s median rent is $2,014—about 16 % of its median income. Both percentages sit comfortably below the national affordability threshold of 30 %, but Frisco’s higher incomes make its rental market feel a bit less burdensome. In short, if you prioritize a larger home‑ownership pie and can shoulder a higher price tag, Frisco’s market may suit you; if you prefer a slightly lower entry cost and a more established, diversified housing stock, Plano offers a competitive alternative.

What Each City Is Known For

Plano is recognized for its established corporate base and its status as the largest city in Collin County. Its history stretches back to the 1840s, and the city has grown around longstanding business districts. Frisco, by contrast, earned national attention as the fastest‑growing U.S. city in 2017 and from 2000 to 2009. It carries the Tree City USA designation since 2003 and promotes a newer, park‑rich environment with 230 sunny days a year.

Who Thrives Best in Each Community

Frisco’s higher incomes, newer housing stock and strong owner‑occupancy rate make it attractive to young professionals and growing families seeking modern amenities and space. Plano’s larger population, slightly lower rent‑to‑income ratio and established corporate presence suit commuters, retirees, and those who value a broader range of services without the premium home price. Both cities offer a suburban lifestyle within easy reach of Dallas, but the choice hinges on budget and the desire for newer versus more established surroundings.

Frequently asked questions

Is Plano or Frisco cheaper to live in?

Plano has lower median rent ($1,841 vs $2,014) and lower home values ($465,900 vs $642,100), making it the more affordable option overall.

Which has higher incomes, Plano or Frisco?

Frisco leads with a median household income of $150,212, compared with Plano’s $112,253.

Is rent higher in Plano or Frisco?

Rent is higher in Frisco at $2,014 per month, while Plano’s median rent is $1,841.

Which is better for families, Plano or Frisco?

Frisco’s higher home‑ownership rate (66%), newer neighborhoods and larger lot sizes often appeal to families, though Plano also offers strong schools and amenities.

Is Plano or Frisco bigger?

Plano is larger by population, with 290,594 residents versus Frisco’s 219,304.

The bottom line

Both Plano and Frisco deliver high incomes, strong home‑ownership and easy access to Dallas. Choose Plano if you prefer a larger, more established city with slightly lower housing costs. Opt for Frisco if higher earnings, newer homes and a family‑focused environment are top priorities.
