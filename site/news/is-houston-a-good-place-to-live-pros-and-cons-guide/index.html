Is Houston a Good Place to Live? Pros and Cons Guide

By Luvoxi News Editorial Team · Nov 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Houston offers a large, diverse city with affordable housing, a strong job market and warm weather. With a population of about 2,328,253 and a median household income of $64,813, it can be an attractive option, but hot summers, flood concerns and a sprawling layout also shape daily life.

Population and Size: How Big Is Houston?

Houston is the most populous city in Texas and the Southern United States, with an estimated 2,328,253 residents according to the 2020-2024 ACS. The 2020 census recorded roughly 2.3 million people. The Greater Houston metropolitan area expands that figure to 7.8 million, ranking it the fifth‑most populous metro area in the nation. Geographically, the city covers 640.4 square miles, making it the ninth largest U.S. city by land area. This combination of size and population creates a wide range of neighborhoods, services and cultural options.

Cost of Living: Housing and Income

The median household income in Houston is $64,813 per year, according to the U.S. Census Bureau’s 2020‑2024 American Community Survey. That figure sits comfortably above the national median, giving many families a solid financial base while still keeping everyday expenses in check. Housing costs reflect this balance: the median gross rent is $1,361 a month, and the median price of an owner‑occupied home is $277,800. Because only about 42 % of occupied housing units are owner‑occupied, a robust rental market exists, offering a range of options from downtown apartments to suburban complexes.

When you compare these numbers to coastal metropolises such as San Francisco or New York, Houston’s cost of living appears markedly lower. A family earning the median income can typically afford a three‑bedroom home in neighborhoods like Katy or Pearland, or a modern one‑bedroom apartment in the Heights, while still having discretionary income for transportation, childcare, and entertainment. The city’s sprawling 640.4 square‑mile footprint means that new development often pushes outward, creating more affordable housing pockets on the edges of the metro area.

Additionally, Houston’s youthful demographic—median age 34.4—helps sustain a dynamic labor market, especially in energy, health care, and technology sectors that tend to pay above‑average wages. Together, these factors make Houston one of the more affordable large‑city choices in the United States, delivering urban amenities without the premium price tag found in many other major hubs.

Climate: What to Expect Year‑Round

Houston’s climate is classified as humid subtropical. Summers are long and hot, with an average of 106.5 days each year reaching 90 °F (32 °C) and about 4.6 days climbing to 100 °F (37.8 °C). Winters are mild, rarely seeing freezing temperatures. The city receives prevailing winds from the south and southeast, bringing heat and tropical moisture from the Gulf of Mexico. This weather pattern supports lush vegetation but also contributes to high humidity and occasional severe storms.

Transportation and Connectivity

Houston’s location near Galveston Bay and the Gulf of Mexico makes it a key port city. Major transportation hubs include George Bush Intercontinental Airport, a global gateway for passengers and cargo, and William P. Hobby Airport, which handles many domestic flights. The city’s extensive highway network links it to Austin (165 miles east), the Louisiana border (88 miles west), and Dallas (250 miles north). Public transit options exist, though the city’s sprawling layout often encourages car use.

Geography and Flood Risk: A Double‑Edged Sword

Houston sits at the historic confluence of Buffalo Bayou and White Oak Bayou, a location that gave the city its name and early economic advantage but also creates a natural low‑lying basin that drains slowly. Spanning 640.4 square miles across Harris, Fort Bend, and Montgomery counties, the metropolis is the ninth‑largest U.S. city by area, yet much of that land is only a few feet above sea level. Prevailing southeast winds sweep warm, moisture‑laden air from the Gulf of Mexico into the region, producing the humid subtropical climate that brings hot, humid summers (averaging 106.5 days ≥ 90 °F) and mild winters. When tropical systems or stalled frontal boundaries dump rain, the bayous can quickly become overwhelmed, as seen in the historic floods of 2015‑2017 and the catastrophic Hurricane Harvey in 2017.

Because flooding directly affects housing, residents and developers have adopted a range of mitigation strategies. Approximately 42 % of the 2.3 million‑strong population lives in owner‑occupied homes, with a median value of $277,800; many of these homes are built on raised foundations or equipped with flood‑resistant utilities. The city has invested in expanding drainage capacity, constructing detention ponds, and updating zoning codes to require higher elevation for new construction. Despite these efforts, the risk remains a daily consideration for renters—who pay a median gross rent of $1,361—and for the city’s younger median age of 34.4 years, who often prioritize affordability over flood‑proofing. Understanding Houston’s geography is therefore essential for anyone weighing the trade‑off between its vibrant, low‑cost urban lifestyle and the persistent threat of water‑related hazards.

Frequently asked questions

What is the population of Houston and its metropolitan area?

Houston has an estimated 2,328,253 residents, while the Greater Houston metro area totals about 7.8 million people, making it the fifth‑largest metro region in the United States.

What is the climate like in Houston throughout the year?

Houston experiences a humid subtropical climate with hot, humid summers—averaging 106.5 days at or above 90 °F and about 4.6 days over 100 °F—and mild winters that rarely drop below freezing.

How large is Houston in terms of land area?

The city spans 640.4 square miles (1,659 km²), ranking it as the ninth largest city by area in the United States.

What geographic factors contribute to flooding in Houston?

Houston’s low‑lying position at the meeting point of several bayous, combined with tropical moisture from the Gulf and prevailing southeast winds, creates conditions that can lead to heavy rainfall and flooding.

The bottom line

Houston balances affordability, a strong economy and cultural depth with hot weather and flood considerations. Weigh these pros and cons against your lifestyle priorities to decide if the city fits your long‑term plans.
