Living in Frisco TX: What New Residents Need to Know

Frisco, Texas, sits in Collin and Denton counties about 25 miles from both Dallas Love Field and DFW International Airport. With a 2020 census population of 200,509, it’s a fast‑growing suburb of the Dallas‑Fort Worth metroplex offering ample retail, a mild climate, and a unique funding model for economic development.

Population and Growth Trends

Frisco’s 2020 census count stood at 200,509 residents, making it one of the largest cities in the Dallas‑Fort Worth area. The city earned the title of fastest‑growing U.S. city in 2017 and maintained rapid expansion from 2000 to 2009. This growth is reflected in the larger McKinney‑Frisco urban area, which reached 504,803 people in 2020, ranking 83rd nationwide. The surge in residents has spurred new housing, schools, and businesses, creating a dynamic environment for newcomers.

Location and Transportation

Frisco lies roughly 25 miles (40 km) north of both Dallas Love Field and Dallas/Fort Worth International Airport, providing easy access for travelers and commuters. The city’s main north‑south artery, Preston Road, is lined with retail and dining options and connects to major highways. While Frisco does not participate in Dallas Area Rapid Transit, it funds its Economic Development Corporation through a fractional percent of local sales tax, a model that differs from many neighboring DFW municipalities.

Climate and Weather Patterns

Frisco experiences a humid subtropical climate, averaging 39 inches (990 mm) of precipitation annually. Residents enjoy about 230 sunny days each year, with July highs reaching 96 °F (36 °C) and January lows dropping to 33 °F (1 °C). The city sits in Tornado Alley; the most recent confirmed tornado was an EF‑0 in 2024 near the UNT Frisco Campus. Seasonal variations are moderate, making outdoor activities feasible for most of the year.

Retail and Shopping Destinations

Shopping in Frisco is anchored by the 165‑store Stonebriar Centre, a regional mall that draws visitors from across the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex. Beyond the usual fashion and electronics retailers, the mall houses a cinema complex, a full‑service spa, and a seasonal indoor farmers market, giving residents a one‑stop destination for both everyday needs and special occasions. Just a short drive east, IKEA’s 310,000‑square‑foot showroom offers everything from Scandinavian‑style furniture to a sprawling kitchen‑display area, making home‑renovation projects feel less like a chore and more like an outing.

The retail corridor along Preston Road amplifies the city’s shopping appeal. This north–south artery is lined with boutique clothing shops, specialty food stores, and an eclectic mix of restaurants ranging from upscale steakhouses to casual taco joints. Because Frisco sits only about 25 miles from both Dallas Love Field and DFW International Airport, the area attracts out‑of‑town shoppers who appreciate the convenience of high‑quality retail without the traffic of downtown Dallas.

In addition to these flagship venues, Frisco’s Economic Development Corporation reinvests a fraction of local sales tax into further retail growth, encouraging new concepts such as pop‑up markets and artisan fairs. The result is a dynamic shopping landscape that reflects the city’s rapid growth—its population surged to 200,509 in the 2020 census—and its commitment to providing residents with diverse, convenient, and locally supported retail experiences—all without leaving the 62‑square‑mile city limits.

Economic Development Funding Model

Unlike many DFW municipalities that lean on regional transit taxes, Frisco finances its Economic Development Corporation (EDC) with a fractional percent of the city’s own sales tax. Each dollar spent at a Frisco retailer contributes a small, earmarked portion—currently 0.1 % of the local sales‑tax base—to a dedicated fund that the city controls directly. Because the money never passes through the Dallas Area Rapid Transit system, the EDC can allocate resources on a quarterly basis to projects that match Frisco’s unique growth trajectory.

The model has paid dividends since the city’s population surged to 200,509 in the 2020 census and the McKinney‑Frisco urban area topped half a million residents. With rapid expansion comes a need for targeted infrastructure: the sales‑tax pool has financed upgrades to Preston Road, added bike lanes near the Stonebriar Centre, and supported the construction of a new transit hub near The Star, home of the Dallas Cowboys headquarters. It also backs incentives that attract national retailers—such as the 310,000‑square‑foot IKEA and the 165‑store Stonebriar Centre—by offering site‑improvement grants and streamlined permitting.

Because the funding is locally sourced, Frisco can match its economic goals with community priorities, from preserving green space (the city has been a Tree City USA since 2003) to funding public‑art installations in new mixed‑use districts. This tailored approach ensures that as the city expands across its 62.4 square‑mile footprint, growth remains purposeful, locally driven, and responsive to the needs of new residents.

Recreation and Community Amenities

Since 2003, Frisco has held the Tree City USA designation, reflecting a commitment to green space and urban forestry. The city’s 62.4 square miles include parks, trails, and the notable Star, home to the Dallas Cowboys headquarters. These amenities, combined with a low water footprint—only 0.92% of the city’s area—offer residents ample opportunities for outdoor recreation and community events.

Living Costs and Housing Overview

While specific price data is not provided, Frisco’s rapid growth and extensive retail and corporate presence suggest a competitive housing market. The city’s proximity to major airports and highways, coupled with its robust economic development funding, supports a range of housing options from new subdivisions to established neighborhoods. Prospective residents should consider the city’s amenities, climate, and transportation links when budgeting for a move.
